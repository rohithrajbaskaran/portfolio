What GRC Actually Is (and Why It's More Than Checkbox Compliance)

Passing an audit and being secure are not the same thing.

Passing an audit and being secure are not the same thing. In September 2013, Target was certified compliant with the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS). About two months later, attackers stole data on 40 million payment cards from its store checkout systems.

Many people new to cybersecurity picture GRC (governance, risk, and compliance) as little more than paperwork, policies, and audits that get in the way of “real” security. That view misses the point. Done well, GRC decides what to protect, how much risk to accept, and whether the controls actually work.

This post covers what each part of GRC means, what “checkbox compliance” looks like, and why mature GRC matters far beyond the audit.

What GRC stands for

GRC combines three connected disciplines that help an organization reach its goals while staying secure and within the law.

Governanceis how decisions get made. It covers policies, roles, accountability, and leadership oversight, such as a security policy approved by executives.
Risk managementis how you decide what matters most. You identify what could go wrong, judge how likely and damaging it is, then reduce, transfer, avoid, or accept it.
Complianceis how you prove you meet your obligations. These include laws like GDPR, industry rules like PCI DSS, and customer demands like SOC 2 reports.

The loop is the point: what compliance finds should change leadership decisions, not just fill an audit folder.

What checkbox compliance looks like

Checkbox compliance treats passing the audit as the goal, instead of as a check on real security. The warning signs are easy to spot:

  • Policies written once for the auditor and never read by staff
  • Controls that exist on paper but are not configured, monitored, or tested
  • A yearly scramble for evidence, followed by months of drift
  • A risk register that only gets updated before an audit
  • Success measured by “did we pass?” instead of “are we protected?”

Two well-known breaches show what happens when the paperwork and the reality drift apart.

Target, 2013: certified, then breached

Target testified that its systems were reviewed and certified PCI compliant in September 2013, according to the Congressional Research Service. On November 12, intruders got in using a vendor’s access. That vendor, HVAC contractor Fazio Mechanical Services, later said its connection was used in the attack.

The attackers planted malware on store registers. They took data on 40 million payment cards, plus personal details on up to 70 million customers. By late 2014, Target had reported $248 million in breach-related expenses.

Target’s security tools detected the intrusion, but no one acted until the Justice Department warned the company in December. A Senate committee staff report said Target failed to act on multiple automated warnings and let a vendor with weak security practices onto its network.

Equifax, 2017: the policy existed, the patch did not

In March 2017, a critical flaw in Apache Struts web software was made public. Equifax’s security team emailed more than 400 people, telling anyone running it to patch within 48 hours, as its own security policy required. According to the FTC, no one followed up to make sure it happened.

An internet-facing dispute portal called ACIS stayed unpatched. Attackers entered through it on May 13 and stayed for 76 days. A device meant to inspect that traffic had been blind for 19 months because its security certificate had expired.

The House Oversight Committee report traced the failure to unclear accountability for IT, which left an “execution gap” between writing policy and following it. The breach exposed data on about 147 million people. Equifax later settled with the FTC, the CFPB, and 50 states and territories for $575 million to $700 million.

The lesson: a compliance check is a snapshot of one day. Security depends on whether controls work every day, and on someone owning them.

Why GRC matters beyond the checkbox

Good GRC makes security a tool for running the business, not just a cost of passing audits. It earns that role in five ways.

It points spending at what matters most. No company can protect everything equally. A risk assessment shows which systems and data would hurt most to lose, so the budget goes there first.

It helps leaders make informed decisions. Executives make risk calls every day, often without realizing it. GRC gives them the facts to reduce, transfer, avoid, or knowingly accept each risk.

It builds customer trust and wins deals. Enterprise buyers send security questionnaires and ask for SOC 2 reports or ISO 27001 certificates. A strong program answers them quickly and helps close sales.

It limits legal and financial exposure. Under GDPR Article 83, breaking core rules such as the processing principles or individuals’ rights can bring fines of up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. The Equifax settlement shows US regulators can impose heavy costs too.

It builds resilience. Incident response, business continuity, and vendor risk reviews are all GRC work. Target’s breach started with a vendor, and Equifax’s monitoring gap hid an attack for months.

What good GRC looks like in practice

Mature GRC is continuous, owned by the business, and focused on whether controls actually work. The difference shows up in everyday habits:

AreaCheckbox complianceMature GRC
PoliciesWritten for the auditor, then filed awayShort, readable, reviewed yearly, and backed by training
Risk registerUpdated the week before an auditReviewed regularly, with a named owner for each risk
EvidenceGathered in a yearly scrambleCollected continuously, often automatically
OwnershipSecurity “owns” every riskBusiness leaders own their risks; security advises
VendorsQuestionnaire at signing, then forgottenAccess limited and reviewed for the life of the contract
Follow-throughAn instruction is sent, and the task is assumed doneFixes are verified, and gaps are escalated to an owner
Key question“Did we pass?”“Are we secure, and can we prove it?”

The goal is not to skip audits. It is to make passing them a side effect of doing security well.

Common frameworks you’ll hear about

Frameworks give GRC teams a shared structure, so nobody has to invent controls from scratch. These five come up most often:

FrameworkWhat it isCommonly used by
NIST CSF 2.0A voluntary set of security outcomes, organized into six functionsOrganizations of any size or sector
ISO/IEC 27001International standard for an information security management system (ISMS), with formal certification availableCompanies selling internationally or needing a certificate
SOC 2An independent auditor’s report on a service provider’s controls, based on the AICPA Trust Services CriteriaSaaS and cloud providers proving security to customers
CIS ControlsA prioritized list of practical security safeguardsTeams wanting a concrete starting point
PCI DSSThe security standard card brands require of businesses that handle payment card dataRetailers, payment processors, and any business taking cards

The NIST update shows where the field is heading. Version 2.0, released in February 2024, added Govern as a sixth function beside Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. NIST now frames cybersecurity as a major enterprise risk for senior leaders, on par with finance and reputation.

Who works in GRC

GRC teams mix people from audit, IT, legal, and business backgrounds. Common roles include:

RoleWhat the work involves
GRC analystMaintains policies, the risk register, and control mappings across frameworks
IT auditorTests whether controls work and documents evidence and findings
Risk analystAssesses new systems, projects, and threats, then recommends how to treat each risk
Compliance analystTracks the laws and standards the company must meet and prepares for audits
Third-party risk analystReviews vendors’ security before and during contracts
Privacy analystManages obligations for personal data under laws like GDPR

The skills that matter most are often not technical:

  • Clear writing: policies, findings, and reports are the core output of the job.
  • Communication: you translate technical risk into business terms for executives.
  • Business sense: you weigh security against cost, speed, and customer impact.
  • Framework knowledge: you know how controls in NIST, ISO 27001, and SOC 2 map to each other.
  • Enough technical depth: you can tell whether a control actually works, not just whether it exists.

Compliance is the floor, not the ceiling

Compliance tells you that you met a minimum standard on the day someone checked. GRC is the ongoing work of deciding what to protect, owning each risk, and proving that controls work every day. Target and Equifax both had security teams and tools, and both learned the difference the hard way.

If you are new to cybersecurity, GRC is a strong place to start. It rewards clear thinking and good communication, and it shows you how security connects to the whole business.
